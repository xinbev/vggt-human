024                                                 ABSTRACT
025
026              Reconstructing humans and their surroundings from monocular video requires re-
027              solving scale ambiguity and recovering their spatial relationships. Even with a
028
                 shared scale, errors in human placement can cause floating and penetration arti-
                 facts. We introduce GroundedHuman, a framework for monocular 4D human–
029
                 scene reconstruction that recovers scene geometry, camera trajectories, and hu-
030
                 man motion in a shared metric coordinate system. Our approach follows hierar-
031              chical spatial grounding: human metric priors establish a global reference, and
032              regional geometry guides human placement within it. We initialize scene scale
033              from human-surface correspondences and use anchor–scene cross-attention to pre-
034              dict residual scale and depth corrections. Aggregating evidence across people and
035              frames yields a common scale for scene depth and camera translation. Within
036              the calibrated scene, we distinguish self-surface and environmental evidence at
037              multiple spatial scales around body regions, combining fine geometric detail with
038              broader context. Reliability-weighted regional predictions then guide iterative up-
039
                 dates to human translation while preserving the estimated body pose and shape.
                 Experiments demonstrate improved world-space human motion and qualitatively
040
                 more consistent human–scene contact.

043   1     INTRODUCTION
044
045   Reconstructing humans and their surroundings from monocular video requires recovering how peo-
046   ple move within a shared physical space. For applications such as augmented reality, embodied
047   perception, and digital human animation, the placement of a body relative to its environment is as
048   important as the geometry of either component. Joint human–scene reconstruction therefore seeks
049   to recover human motion, scene geometry, and camera trajectories in a common world coordinate
050
      system, with consistent physical dimensions and spatial relationships (Chen et al., 2026; Li et al.,
      2026; Liu et al., 2026).
051
052   Two challenges arise when bringing these estimates together. Scene reconstruction models recover
053   geometry and camera motion with uncertain metric scale, whereas parametric human estimators pro-
      vide metric body estimates based on learned human priors (Wang et al., 2025a; 2026a; Sárándi &


                                                         1
      Under review as a conference paper at ICLR 2027




054
      Pons-Moll, 2024). A mismatch between these references affects scene dimensions, camera transla-
055   tion, and world-space human motion. Even at a common scale, errors in human position can leave
056   feet floating above the ground or bodies intersecting nearby surfaces. Occlusion and incomplete ge-
057   ometry further obscure the spatial evidence needed to correct these errors. Coherent reconstruction
058   thus requires both a shared metric reference and accurate human placement within that reference.
059
      Recent work has advanced both joint reconstruction and interaction-aware refinement. Human3R
060
      jointly recovers humans, scenes, and cameras, while UniSH combines human and scene features
061   with geometric correspondence supervision for metric reconstruction and human placement (Chen
062   et al., 2026; Li et al., 2026). UniCon3R uses predicted contact to correct human reconstruction, and
063   GRAFT learns iterative human-parameter updates from nearby scene geometry (Sur et al., 2026;
064   Pradyumna YM et al., 2026). These approaches demonstrate the value of connecting human and
065   scene estimates. A central diﬀiculty in interpreting their geometric relationship, however, is that a
066   local discrepancy may reflect scene-scale error, human-position error, or an unreliable observation.
067   Correcting human placement therefore depends on both the metric reference and the local evidence
068   used to assess alignment.
069   We address this diﬀiculty through hierarchical spatial grounding: human anchors establish a
070   global metric reference, and regional human–environment geometry guides the refinement of hu-
071   man placement within it. This principle underlies GroundedHuman, our framework for monocular
072   4D human–scene reconstruction. Establishing the metric reference first makes local geometric dis-
073   crepancies comparable before they are used to correct human position.
074   Visible human surfaces provide a direct starting point for metric calibration: their estimated depths
075   can be compared with corresponding scene observations to obtain an initial scale. Such correspon-
076   dences are imperfect, however, because occlusion, depth errors, and inaccurate human placement
077   can bias the estimate. We therefore combine this analytic initialization with learned residual cali-
078   bration that relates body anchors to nearby scene evidence. The initialization establishes the coarse
079   scale, allowing the learned correction to focus on the remaining discrepancies in their local context.
080   Observations from different people and frames provide complementary support for a shared scale;
081
      aggregating them through temporal consensus reduces reliance on any individual estimate. Applying
      the recovered scale to both scene depth and camera translation establishes a common metric reference
082
      for subsequent placement refinement.
083
084   Within this calibrated scene, improving human placement requires interpreting geometry around
085   the body. A narrow neighborhood reveals fine alignment errors but may provide limited evidence
086   about surrounding surfaces, while a broader neighborhood supplies context for understanding the
087
      body’s relation to the environment. We therefore gather geometry at multiple spatial scales around
      anatomically corresponding body regions. We also distinguish observations of the person’s own
088
      surface from environmental evidence, since agreement with the former and spatial relationships to
089
      the latter provide different cues for placement. Combining these cues yields regional corrections
090   whose contributions depend on their reliability. The resulting iterative updates adjust the position of
091   the body as a whole, improving human–scene alignment and contact consistency while preserving
092   its estimated pose and shape.
093
      Our contributions are summarized as follows:
094
095
             • We introduce GroundedHuman, a framework for monocular 4D human–scene reconstruc-
096            tion through hierarchical spatial grounding. It addresses global scale mismatch and local
097            contact misalignment, recovering humans, scenes, and camera trajectories in a shared met-
098            ric coordinate system with improved human–scene contact consistency.
099
             • We propose Human-Anchored Metric Attention for coarse-to-fine metric calibration. It
100            combines human-surface scale initialization with anchor–scene cross-attention to estimate
101            residual scale and depth corrections, using the calibrated scale consistently for scene depth
102            and camera translation.
103
             • We develop Multi-Scale Regional Interaction Attention to improve human placement
104            and contact consistency. It distinguishes self-surface and environmental evidence across
105            spatial scales, combining local geometric detail with surrounding context to iteratively refine
106            human translation through reliability-weighted regional predictions while preserving body
107            pose and shape.

      2   RELATED WORK
109
110
      3D Scene Reconstruction Recent visual geometry foundation models have enabled direct predic-
111   tion of scene geometry and camera motion from RGB observations. DUSt3R estimates pixel-aligned
112   3D pointmaps, while MASt3R introduces dense feature matching to establish cross-view correspon-
113   dences (Wang et al., 2024a; Leroy et al., 2024). VGGT jointly predicts camera parameters, depth, and
114   dense geometry, whereas π 3 achieves reference-free reconstruction through permutation-equivariant
115   geometric prediction (Wang et al., 2025a;c). Extending reconstruction to dynamic and sequential
116   observations, MonST3R models geometry in the presence of motion, CUT3R maintains a persistent
117   state for continuous scene reconstruction, and VGGT-Ω advances feed-forward geometry estimation
118   for both static and dynamic scenes (Zhang et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2025b; 2026a). Despite these
119
      advances, monocular scene reconstruction remains subject to scale ambiguity, while moving people
      introduce occlusion and depth discontinuities. Consequently, scene geometry and independently re-
120
      constructed human bodies may lack a consistent metric scale, leading to inaccurate spatial alignment.
121
122
      Human Reconstruction and Global Motion Human Mesh Recovery (HMR) estimates 3D hu-
123
      man pose and shape, commonly using parametric body models such as SMPL and SMPL-X (Loper
124   et al., 2023; Pavlakos et al., 2019). HMR 2.0 and Multi-HMR recover expressive human meshes from
125   monocular images, while NLF employs a continuous representation for dense human pose and shape
126   estimation (Goel et al., 2023; Baradel et al., 2024; Sárándi & Pons-Moll, 2024). However, accurate
127   local body reconstruction does not guarantee correct global placement. World-grounded HMR meth-
128   ods further recover human motion in a shared world coordinate frame. GLAMR uses learned motion
129   priors to recover global trajectories under occlusion, while SLAHMR combines relative camera mo-
130   tion with human motion priors for global reconstruction (Yuan et al., 2022; Ye et al., 2023). TRAM
131   leverages metric depth for camera calibration, WHAM combines motion context with contact-aware
132
      trajectory refinement, and GVHMR introduces gravity-view coordinates for world-grounded motion
      recovery (Wang et al., 2024b; Shin et al., 2024; Shen et al., 2024). More recently, DuoMo separates
133
      camera-space and world-space motion generation into two diffusion stages (Wang et al., 2026b).
134
      Nevertheless, temporally coherent human motion does not ensure accurate placement within the sur-
135   rounding scene, as global localization errors can persist without suﬀicient constraints from scene
136   geometry.
137
138   Scene-Human Reconstruction Recent human–scene reconstruction methods increasingly exploit
139   human priors and scene geometry for joint reconstruction. SynCHMR uses human-aware metric
140   SLAM to recover metric camera poses and scene geometry, followed by scene-aware human motion
141   refinement (Zhao et al., 2024). MetricHMSR incorporates camera-ray representations for metric
142   human recovery and uses reconstructed human surfaces as geometric anchors to refine monocular
143   scene depth (Song et al., 2026). JOSH jointly optimizes camera poses, scene geometry, and hu-
144
      man motion through correspondence and contact constraints (Liu et al., 2026). These approaches
      establish metric consistency through human priors or joint optimization, while recent feed-forward
145
      methods integrate human and scene reasoning directly into geometric prediction. HAMSt3R aug-
146
      ments multi-view reconstruction with human segmentation and dense body correspondences, while
147   Human3R incorporates human prompts into a recurrent geometry model to jointly recover scene
148   structure, camera motion, and global human meshes (Rojas et al., 2025; Chen et al., 2026). UniSH
149   further combines scene reconstruction and HMR priors with explicit human–scene geometric corre-
150   spondence for joint metric-scale reconstruction (Li et al., 2026). However, metric-scale reconstruc-
151   tion and joint geometric prediction do not fully resolve human–scene interpenetration and floating
152   artifacts, particularly under occlusion and ambiguous depth. To improve physical consistency, Uni-
153   Con3R feeds predicted human–scene contact back into human reconstruction as a corrective signal
154   (Sur et al., 2026), while GRAFT employs body-anchored geometric probes and recurrent refinement
155
      to correct human meshes according to nearby scene surfaces (Pradyumna YM et al., 2026). Al-
      though geometric feedback improves human–scene alignment, jointly refining body parameters may
156
      introduce unnecessary changes to local articulation when correcting global placement errors. This
157
      highlights the challenge of improving human–scene consistency while preserving reliable local body
158   pose and shape.

506   5   CONCLUSION
507
508   We presented GroundedHuman, a framework that connects metric human priors with relative scene
509   geometry to reconstruct humans, scenes, and camera motion from monocular video. Scale calibra-
510   tion establishes a shared metric reference for scene depth and camera translation, while regional
511
      translation refinement uses local geometric evidence to adjust human placement without changing
      pose or shape. Evaluations of world-space motion, scene depth, and local human meshes, together
512
      with qualitative comparisons, examine complementary aspects of this reconstruction: body structure,
513
      movement, and placement within the environment. The framework makes scene scale and human
514   position explicit calibration variables while retaining the local geometry supplied by pretrained es-
515   timators. Future work will explore temporal evidence aggregation and contact constraints to extend
516   this formulation to longer sequences and more complex interactions.
517
518   Limitations. GroundedHuman depends on the quality and visibility of the initial human estimates
519   and on the accuracy of scene depth. Biased body shape or root position can affect scale calibration,
520   while occlusion and local depth errors can weaken the evidence for translation refinement. Without
521   reliable human observations, the metric scale is weakly constrained. Preserving pose and shape
522   prevents global alignment errors from being absorbed by local deformation, but also leaves incorrect
523   articulation and body shape unchanged. Translation alone cannot resolve all human–scene contact
524
      errors, particularly when these require changes in pose or scene geometry.

869         5. Run the person-conditioned residual aﬀine module to obtain residual scale and depth bias.
870         6. Aggregate the shared scale within the clip and propagate it to depth and camera translation.
871         7. Run two TRSTR iterations, updating only each person’s root translation.
            8. Output humans, scene geometry, and camera trajectories in a shared world coordinate system.

955   Figures 3, 5, and 6 show temporal reconstruction, human placement, and selected length measure-
956   ments in the main paper. Camera and oracle diagnostics appear in Fig. 4 of Sec. 4.2. These examples
957   do not replace controlled robustness or failure-rate evaluation.
958   The main failure chains include insuﬀicient anchor support caused by invisibility or severe trunca-

960   by VGGT-Ω relative-structure errors; failed environment/other-human separation under dynamic ob-
961   jects or multi-person occlusion; and the inability of translation-only TRSTR to correct pose, shape,
962   or fine-grained contact.
