ABSTRACT
Reconstructing humans and their surroundings from monocular video requires re-
solving scale ambiguity and recovering their spatial relationships. Even with a
shared scale, errors in human placement can cause floating and penetration arti-
facts. We introduce GroundedHuman, a framework for monocular 4D human–
scene reconstruction that recovers scene geometry, camera trajectories, and hu-
man motion in a shared metric coordinate system. Our approach follows hierar-
chical spatial grounding: human metric priors establish a global reference, and
regional geometry guides human placement within it. We initialize scene scale
from human-surface correspondences and use anchor–scene cross-attention to pre-
dict residual scale and depth corrections. Aggregating evidence across people and
frames yields a common scale for scene depth and camera translation. Within
the calibrated scene, we distinguish self-surface and environmental evidence at
multiple spatial scales around body regions, combining fine geometric detail with
broader context. Reliability-weighted regional predictions then guide iterative up-
dates to human translation while preserving the estimated body pose and shape.
Experiments demonstrate improved world-space human motion and qualitatively
more consistent human–scene contact.
1 INTRODUCTION
Reconstructing humans and their surroundings from monocular video requires recovering how peo-
ple move within a shared physical space. For applications such as augmented reality, embodied
perception, and digital human animation, the placement of a body relative to its environment is as
important as the geometry of either component. Joint human–scene reconstruction therefore seeks
to recover human motion, scene geometry, and camera trajectories in a common world coordinate
system, with consistent physical dimensions and spatial relationships ( Chen et al. , 2026; Li et al. ,
2026; Liu et al. , 2026).

geometry and camera motion with uncertain metric scale, whereas parametric human estimators pro-
vide metric body estimates based on learned human priors ( Wang et al. , 2025a; 2026a; Sárándi &

Pons-Moll , 2024). A mismatch between these references affects scene dimensions, camera transla-
tion, and world-space human motion. Even at a common scale, errors in human position can leave
feet floating above the ground or bodies intersecting nearby surfaces. Occlusion and incomplete ge-
ometry further obscure the spatial evidence needed to correct these errors. Coherent reconstruction
thus requires both a shared metric reference and accurate human placement within that reference.
Recent work has advanced both joint reconstruction and interaction-aware refinement. Human3R
jointly recovers humans, scenes, and cameras, while UniSH combines human and scene features
with geometric correspondence supervision for metric reconstruction and human placement ( Chen
et al., 2026; Li et al. , 2026). UniCon3R uses predicted contact to correct human reconstruction, and
GRAFT learns iterative human-parameter updates from nearby scene geometry ( Sur et al. , 2026;
Pradyumna YM et al. , 2026). These approaches demonstrate the value of connecting human and
scene estimates. A central diﬀiculty in interpreting their geometric relationship, however, is that a
local discrepancy may reflect scene-scale error, human-position error, or an unreliable observation.
Correcting human placement therefore depends on both the metric reference and the local evidence
used to assess alignment.
We address this diﬀiculty through hierarchical spatial grounding : human anchors establish a
global metric reference, and regional human–environment geometry guides the refinement of hu-
man placement within it. This principle underlies GroundedHuman, our framework for monocular
4D human–scene reconstruction. Establishing the metric reference first makes local geometric dis-
crepancies comparable before they are used to correct human position.
Visible human surfaces provide a direct starting point for metric calibration: their estimated depths
can be compared with corresponding scene observations to obtain an initial scale. Such correspon-
dences are imperfect, however, because occlusion, depth errors, and inaccurate human placement
can bias the estimate. We therefore combine this analytic initialization with learned residual cali-
bration that relates body anchors to nearby scene evidence. The initialization establishes the coarse
scale, allowing the learned correction to focus on the remaining discrepancies in their local context.
Observations from different people and frames provide complementary support for a shared scale;
aggregating them through temporal consensus reduces reliance on any individual estimate. Applying
the recovered scale to both scene depth and camera translation establishes a common metric reference
for subsequent placement refinement.
Within this calibrated scene, improving human placement requires interpreting geometry around
the body. A narrow neighborhood reveals fine alignment errors but may provide limited evidence
about surrounding surfaces, while a broader neighborhood supplies context for understanding the
body’s relation to the environment. We therefore gather geometry at multiple spatial scales around
anatomically corresponding body regions. We also distinguish observations of the person’s own
surface from environmental evidence, since agreement with the former and spatial relationships to
the latter provide different cues for placement. Combining these cues yields regional corrections
whose contributions depend on their reliability. The resulting iterative updates adjust the position of
the body as a whole, improving human–scene alignment and contact consistency while preserving
its estimated pose and shape.
Our contributions are summarized as follows:
• We introduce GroundedHuman, a framework for monocular 4D human–scene reconstruc-
tion through hierarchical spatial grounding. It addresses global scale mismatch and local
contact misalignment, recovering humans, scenes, and camera trajectories in a shared met-
ric coordinate system with improved human–scene contact consistency.
• We propose Human-Anchored Metric Attention for coarse-to-fine metric calibration. It
combines human-surface scale initialization with anchor–scene cross-attention to estimate
residual scale and depth corrections, using the calibrated scale consistently for scene depth
and camera translation.
• We develop Multi-Scale Regional Interaction Attention to improve human placement
and contact consistency. It distinguishes self-surface and environmental evidence across
spatial scales, combining local geometric detail with surrounding context to iteratively refine
human translation through reliability-weighted regional predictions while preserving body
pose and shape.

2 RELATED WORK
3D Scene Reconstruction Recent visual geometry foundation models have enabled direct predic-
tion of scene geometry and camera motion from RGB observations. DUSt3R estimates pixel-aligned
3D pointmaps, while MASt3R introduces dense feature matching to establish cross-view correspon-
dences (Wang et al. , 2024a; Leroy et al., 2024). VGGT jointly predicts camera parameters, depth, and
dense geometry, whereas π3 achieves reference-free reconstruction through permutation-equivariant
geometric prediction ( Wang et al. , 2025a;c). Extending reconstruction to dynamic and sequential
observations, MonST3R models geometry in the presence of motion, CUT3R maintains a persistent
state for continuous scene reconstruction, and VGGT- Ω advances feed-forward geometry estimation
for both static and dynamic scenes ( Zhang et al. , 2024; Wang et al. , 2025b; 2026a). Despite these
advances, monocular scene reconstruction remains subject to scale ambiguity, while moving people
introduce occlusion and depth discontinuities. Consequently, scene geometry and independently re-
constructed human bodies may lack a consistent metric scale, leading to inaccurate spatial alignment.
Human Reconstruction and Global Motion Human Mesh Recovery (HMR) estimates 3D hu-
man pose and shape, commonly using parametric body models such as SMPL and SMPL-X ( Loper
et al., 2023; Pavlakos et al., 2019). HMR 2.0 and Multi-HMR recover expressive human meshes from
monocular images, while NLF employs a continuous representation for dense human pose and shape
estimation (Goel et al. , 2023; Baradel et al. , 2024; Sárándi & Pons-Moll , 2024). However, accurate
local body reconstruction does not guarantee correct global placement. World-grounded HMR meth-
ods further recover human motion in a shared world coordinate frame. GLAMR uses learned motion
priors to recover global trajectories under occlusion, while SLAHMR combines relative camera mo-
tion with human motion priors for global reconstruction ( Yuan et al. , 2022; Y e et al., 2023). TRAM
leverages metric depth for camera calibration, WHAM combines motion context with contact-aware
trajectory refinement, and GVHMR introduces gravity-view coordinates for world-grounded motion
recovery (Wang et al. , 2024b; Shin et al. , 2024; Shen et al. , 2024). More recently, DuoMo separates
camera-space and world-space motion generation into two diffusion stages ( Wang et al. , 2026b).
Nevertheless, temporally coherent human motion does not ensure accurate placement within the sur-
rounding scene, as global localization errors can persist without suﬀicient constraints from scene
geometry.
Scene-Human Reconstruction Recent human–scene reconstruction methods increasingly exploit
human priors and scene geometry for joint reconstruction. SynCHMR uses human-aware metric
SLAM to recover metric camera poses and scene geometry, followed by scene-aware human motion
refinement ( Zhao et al. , 2024). MetricHMSR incorporates camera-ray representations for metric
human recovery and uses reconstructed human surfaces as geometric anchors to refine monocular
scene depth ( Song et al. , 2026). JOSH jointly optimizes camera poses, scene geometry, and hu-
man motion through correspondence and contact constraints ( Liu et al. , 2026). These approaches
establish metric consistency through human priors or joint optimization, while recent feed-forward
methods integrate human and scene reasoning directly into geometric prediction. HAMSt3R aug-
ments multi-view reconstruction with human segmentation and dense body correspondences, while
Human3R incorporates human prompts into a recurrent geometry model to jointly recover scene
structure, camera motion, and global human meshes ( Rojas et al. , 2025; Chen et al. , 2026). UniSH
further combines scene reconstruction and HMR priors with explicit human–scene geometric corre-
spondence for joint metric-scale reconstruction ( Li et al. , 2026). However, metric-scale reconstruc-
tion and joint geometric prediction do not fully resolve human–scene interpenetration and floating
artifacts, particularly under occlusion and ambiguous depth. To improve physical consistency, Uni-
Con3R feeds predicted human–scene contact back into human reconstruction as a corrective signal
(Sur et al. , 2026), while GRAFT employs body-anchored geometric probes and recurrent refinement
to correct human meshes according to nearby scene surfaces ( Pradyumna YM et al. , 2026). Al-
though geometric feedback improves human–scene alignment, jointly refining body parameters may
introduce unnecessary changes to local articulation when correcting global placement errors. This
highlights the challenge of improving human–scene consistency while preserving reliable local body
pose and shape.

5 CONCLUSION
We presented GroundedHuman, a framework that connects metric human priors with relative scene
geometry to reconstruct humans, scenes, and camera motion from monocular video. Scale calibra-
tion establishes a shared metric reference for scene depth and camera translation, while regional
translation refinement uses local geometric evidence to adjust human placement without changing
pose or shape. Evaluations of world-space motion, scene depth, and local human meshes, together
with qualitative comparisons, examine complementary aspects of this reconstruction: body structure,
movement, and placement within the environment. The framework makes scene scale and human
position explicit calibration variables while retaining the local geometry supplied by pretrained es-
timators. Future work will explore temporal evidence aggregation and contact constraints to extend
this formulation to longer sequences and more complex interactions.
Limitations. GroundedHuman depends on the quality and visibility of the initial human estimates
and on the accuracy of scene depth. Biased body shape or root position can affect scale calibration,
while occlusion and local depth errors can weaken the evidence for translation refinement. Without
reliable human observations, the metric scale is weakly constrained. Preserving pose and shape
prevents global alignment errors from being absorbed by local deformation, but also leaves incorrect
articulation and body shape unchanged. Translation alone cannot resolve all human–scene contact
errors, particularly when these require changes in pose or scene geometry.

C.6 INFERENCE ALGORITHM
1. Run the frozen VGGT-Ω to obtain relative depth, cameras, and scene features.
2. Run the frozen NLF to obtain local humans and confidence values.
3. Project the visible SMPL front surface and compute person/frame scale proposals.
4. Apply robust aggregation to obtain the analytic coarse scale.
5. Run the person-conditioned residual aﬀine module to obtain residual scale and depth bias.
6. Aggregate the shared scale within the clip and propagate it to depth and camera translation.
7. Run two TRSTR iterations, updating only each person’s root translation.
8. Output humans, scene geometry, and camera trajectories in a shared world coordinate system.

Scale calibration and TRSTR are trained separately. The scale stage learns residual aﬀine param-

perturbed human–scene states. VGGT- Ω and the human observer are not jointly optimized with
these objectives. At inference, neither module receives ground-truth depth, camera motion, or hu-
man parameters.

D.2 COMPLETE LOSSES AND WEIGHTS
The scale-stage objective supervises the residual log-scale, depth bias, and calibrated depth:
Lscale = λsLlog -scale + λbLbias + λdLdepth. (17)
Training uses metric BEDLAM depth and SMPL observations. After an initial residual-scale train-
ing stage, the accepted scale-head training path perturbs metric depth by an absolute scale factor,
estimates an analytic initialization from the perturbed depth, and injects an additional initialization
error. The residual target is the ratio between the required absolute correction and the initialization
actually used. Identity and non-identity perturbation groups expose the heads to both correct and in-
accurate initial scales. Frames without valid scale supervision are excluded from the scale and depth
losses. The bias target is zero in this training path; a general learned correction for arbitrary additive
depth errors is not established by that supervision alone.

states. Translation perturbations act along the camera ray and tangent directions. The training ob-
jective is
LTRSTR = λvoteLvote + λtransLtrans + λgateLgate + λregLreg
+ λstrongLstrong + λidLidentity + λsafeLno-worse + λmonoLmonotonic. (18)

D.3 TRAINING STAGES AND FREEZING STRATEGY
In the accepted scale-head training record, only the residual scale and bias heads are updated; the
scene backbone, human observer, HSI interaction backbone, and body-parameter update branches re-
main frozen. During TRSTR training, the scale module and upstream observers remain frozen, while
the regional features, translation votes, uncertainty estimates, and gates are trained. The geometric
output being corrected is root translation, not pose or shape.

F ADDITIONAL QUALITATIVE RESULTS, FAILURE CASES, AND RESPONSIBLE USE
Figures 3, 5, and 6 show temporal reconstruction, human placement, and selected length measure-
ments in the main paper. Camera and oracle diagnostics appear in Fig. 4 of Sec. 4.2. These examples
do not replace controlled robustness or failure-rate evaluation.
The main failure chains include insuﬀicient anchor support caused by invisibility or severe trunca-
tion; biased metric priors caused by incorrect SMPL shape or height; unreliable local probes caused
by VGGT-Ω relative-structure errors; failed environment/other-human separation under dynamic ob-
jects or multi-person occlusion; and the inability of translation-only TRSTR to correct pose, shape,
or fine-grained contact.
These conditions motivate further evaluation of failure detection and reliability under missing or
misleading geometric evidence.
Human-video reconstruction may be misused for privacy-sensitive behavioral analysis or surveil-
lance. This work is limited to geometric reconstruction and does not perform identity recognition or
sensitive-attribute inference. Canonical human dimensions may also produce systematic scale bias
for children, uncommon body types, or populations outside the training distribution; quantifying this
bias requires a dedicated sensitivity evaluation that is not supplied by the current results.
